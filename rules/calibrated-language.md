# Rule: Calibrated Language

The fastest way to discredit a risk or forensic finding is to over-claim. Precision
about confidence is the discipline that makes this skill trustworthy. Apply these
labels consistently and visibly.

## The three confidence levels

- **Confirmed** — Directly observed on-chain and independently verifiable. "Mint
  authority is set to <addr>" is confirmed; anyone can check it.
- **Probable** — A strong circumstantial pattern, but not proof. "These five wallets
  were funded by one source minutes before launch, so the distribution is probably
  coordinated" — strong inference, still labeled.
- **Possible** — Worth checking, not established. "Volume *might* be wash-traded;
  confirming requires counterparty analysis."

## Rules of use

1. Never present an inference as an observation. "Funds moved to X" (observed) is not
   "X is the scammer" (inference). Keep them separate sentences with separate labels.
2. State what would raise the confidence level — what evidence would move a "possible"
   to "confirmed". This makes findings actionable.
3. On-chain control is not real-world identity. Wallet clustering shows *likely common
   control*, never proven personhood. Say so when it matters.
4. Prefer a narrow confirmed claim over a broad unproven one. "The trail ends at a CEX
   deposit address; on-chain identity is not determinable" is more credible and more
   useful than naming a suspect.
5. Absence of evidence is not evidence of safety. "No adverse signals observed" must
   name what was and wasn't checked.

## Why

Calibrated language is what lets a human trust the output enough to act on it, and
what protects real people from false accusations and false reassurance alike. It is
also what distinguishes this skill from the confident-but-wrong "AI slop" the
ecosystem is flooded with.
